									LIBERIAN COLLOQUIAL – THE LANGUAGE OF OUR PEOPLE								
					February 29, 2012 in Uncategorized
				Contributed by Dogba K. Norris, Jr.
The little West African Nation of Liberia with a population of a little over three and a half million has gained a reputation for being infamously unique throughout the sub-region. Surrounded by so many nations whose culture and economies bear the mark of their former Colonial Masters, the French and British in particular, Liberia, the Grandmother of all others when it comes to advocating for freedom in all its forms, has succeeded in more ways than one in reflecting the dazzling effects of early independence on Liberian life style, culture, religion, trade and even politics.
Key amongst our unique attributes within the world and sub-region is our Speech. Liberians the world over have been able to identify their fellow countrymen in foreign hotel lobbies, airport terminals, shopping malls and other public domains just by listening. Yes, you read me right; listening and instantly recognizing the one thing we all know so well, our own “Local Liberian English” (Liberian Colloquial). Those of you reading this with traveling experience outside of Liberia would bear witness to this. Usually it’s quite a hilarious experience to see a certain Liberian expressing himself to a very confused audience who at most times have no Idea of what he’s saying.  You might hear from the stranger: ‘What is he saying? I don’t understand.  Is he/she speaking French?’
According to the free online dictionary COLLOQUIAL or Colloquial is defined as: “Characteristic of or appropriate to the spoken language or to writing that seeks the effect of speech; informal”.  To be honest I think Liberians have given a whole new meaning to that definition.
As a Liberian based in Ghana, I’ve found myself most times coming to the rescue in such situations. I remember vividly an incident about two months ago when I entered the main departure hall at the Kotoka International Airport in Accra; while on my way home for vacation. I came across a Liberian business woman trying so desperately to explain herself to the Ghanaian Customs Officer the best way she knew how. She was so surprised that even though she explained everything from genesis to revelation the customs officers kept staring at her as though she was from outer space.  This gave me a good laugh as I watched the drama unfold. As the poor lady became frustrated I approached her.
“ Olma wettin happen?” ( What’s the Matter) I asked. She turned with a sigh of relief in her eyes.
”Oh yes tanq God”( Thank God). You Liberian too?  She asked.  “Dis pepoo Jeh givin pepoo hard time my son”( These People just don’t seem to be reasonable my son.)
It was imperative that I provide the magic formula, speak in Liberian English.
“These People Naah understanding you Ma. Please, you gah to talk slow. (They don’t understand you, Madam. You need to speak slowly) “You torkin too fast for Dem” (You’re speaking to fast for them), I added.”
“Ehn Hehn, I see nah”, she responded; “Tanq u yah, my son.  Jeh tork to them for me. Me Pa I tayah Nah”.( Aha, now I understand why, please can you speak to them for me, I’m tired).
I looked up with a broad smile at the confused Customs Officer who had a stupefied look on his face, trying hard to hold back the laughter choking me from inside. As is expected, he was lost; he had not understood a single word of what was just said.
“What did she say?”  He asked.
“Da Fiyah!” retorted. Now he was even more confused.
“Boss, please, I don’t understand” he pleaded in his heavy British accent. In his confusion he even asked wish country we were from forgetting he was holding our Passports.
Origin & Evolution of Colloquial
Today, it is a de facto assumption that Colloquial has successfully permeated the Liberian society. It is now the preferred means of communication for Liberians both at home and abroad. This wasn’t always the case. If we were to take a journey down memory lane to at least two decades ago, speaking Colloquial was almost a taboo. It was clearly the language of the uneducated, the uncultured, a language associated with the residues of a very conventional society.
“Young man, speak good English. Pronounce your words” came my dear mother’s voice as she assumed the role of home tutor.  She reminded me every time I skipped an “s’ or swallowed a “t”.  “Don’t speak like a hoodlum,” she would assert.
Like our present generation, Liberian Colloquial is a Language that has evolved. It has been affected by the same elements that gave rise to the current standard of Liberian life style. The key reason being war and all the other vices that come along with it. Though they seem poles apart Liberian Colloquial is derived from American English as introduced by the Settler’s or Americo-Liberians in the  early 1800’s. Like its predecessor Liberian Colloquial allows the use of words or expressions from other Languages, be it foreign or local. Ironically, these very rules do not fit well with standard American English which usually downplays the use of slangs and other informal tools of communication. Also standard English dictates that words should be pronounced as they are spelled; the only exception being if the word was borrowed from another Language , (e.g. French), it should maintain its originality with distinct enunciation.
In standard English words ending with consonants i.e ( S, T, M, etc.) or letters from the English Alphabet that are not vowels must be pronounced with emphasis placed on the last letter. (e.g Relationship, Government , etc.) Failure to do so will make the statement sound miserable, utterly unpleasant to the listener and could well be a yard stick to determine the eloquence and educational orientation of the speaker.  Fortunately this is not the case with Colloquial.
I have listed a few reasons that are in my view the fundamental reasons why Colloquial has and continues to become popular as the preferred Language in both formal and informal conversations for all Liberians irrespective of creed, tribe, age, or social status.
Liberian Colloquial allows borderless freedom of expression, it requires no academic knowledge of the rules of communication, pronunciation rules do not hold in Colloquial; therefore no formal schooling is required. Considering the fact that Liberia’s Literacy rate now stands at 80% this attribute is most convenient.
As it is derived from American English, which is ultimately spoken at a faster pace without an accent, as opposed to British English; which is slow and otherwise intolerant to any hints of deviating from the Status Quo like the British themselves; the pace and flow with which Colloquial is spoken, makes it almost impossible for a British or French Oriented listener to grasp least to mention keep up with what is being said; leaving the beleaguered listener with a bemused expression on his/her face. Hence it is a way to keep eaves-droppers away once Liberians in a foreign environment, educated or not decide to communicate, as non-Liberians just don’t understand Liberian Colloquial even if they understand standard American English.
There is NO DICTIONARY. There are no Rules when it comes to writing Liberian Colloquial. Spelling words in Liberian Coloquial is the sole prerogative of the writer. How a word is pronounced determines how it is spelled. Since there are variations in pronunciations, hence, there are variations in spelling. Eg. STANDARD WORD: RELATIONSHIP / Colloquial VERSION: RELAYSHONSHAY /RELAYSHUNSHIP. Writing Colloquial at first seemed quite a laughable idea, but with the emergence of texting, chatting and other features of social networking, writing Colloquial has almost become an obsession.  A most hilarious experience I must add. The creativity is fascinating as everyone becomes or assumes the role of a wordsmith.
Colloquial has now become Synonymous to the term “True Liberian” a “Grass Rooter” as politicians would call it. In order to speak and understand Liberian Colloquial, one must be familiar with the terms, slangs or expressions that are usually associated with all sectors of the Liberian Society.(E.g Standard Word: Football/Soccer Colloquial: Tabellah) A listener with no knowledge of what Tabellah means in this context is simply out of the conversation. Because of its status as the language of choice by so many Liberians irrespective of tribe, social or academic station, religion and or age, mainstream orthodox institutions have adopted this means of communication in order to appeal to the majority of listeners. Political institutions, media houses, social welfare organizations, and some high profile and highly educated personalities have joined the Colloquial bandwagon, coining slogans or adopting them for their own use. Even social media groups, e.g.  FaceBook, with its Liberian Colloquial users membership of well over 3,000 Liberians and counting from all around the globe all seeking to keep in touch with their unique culture as epitomized in the use of Liberian Colloquial.
The educational divide coupled with the fact that we as Liberians have no common Language that is understood and spoken by all Liberians irrespective of whence we came (as twi is to Ghanaians), leaves little doubt as to why Colloquial has slowly and steadily assumed the role of a Common Denominator. It is a Language for us and by us, Liberians only.  This is no birthright. It is a by-product of interacting with and understanding the true Liberian mind, the thought and speech pattern of our people, how we reason and why we reason the way we do.
I foresee a string of debates on the increasing use of Colloquial as it enjoys its meteoric rise in our society. Is it a sign of our unique commonality as a people that we often turn a blind eye to? How can we harness the positive energy and a sense of Love and common understanding amongst our kinsmen that Colloquial affords us and channel it into a vehicle to foster National Unity? Does the rise of Colloquial reflect the view that the majority of Liberians just cannot bring themselves to speak sandard English in their day to day Interactions? Or is it that Its prevalence is due to the fact that even the highly educated ( Book People) usually ditch standard English for a Language that has less rules, more freedom and is much more common in our Liberian setting? Is the emergence of Colloquial good for our society or is it bad? Will it ruin the next generation and thus should be forbidden like a plague?
Well, you be the Judge. ( Na u be dey jodge)
I can tell you this for sure; Liberian COLLOQUIAL is here to stay. Whether one chooses to speak, tolerate, or forbid, Colloquial is ultimately a matter of personal choice.
Thanks (Yor tanqu)

		SOME WISE WORDS
			I WISH YOU ENOUGH
Recently, I overheard a mother and daughter in their last moments together at the airport as the daughter’s departure had been announced.  Standing near the security gate, they hugged and the mother said:
“I love you and I wish you enough.”
The daughter replied, “Mom, our life together has been more than enough.  Your love is all I ever needed.  I wish you enough, too, Mom.”  They kissed and the daughter left.
The mother walked over to the window where I sat.  Standing there, I could see she wanted and needed to cry.
I tried not to intrude on her privacy but she welcomed me in by asking, “Did you ever say good-bye to someone knowing it would be forever?” “Yes, I have,” I replied.  “Forgive me for asking but why is this is a forever good-bye?”
“I am old and she lives so far away.  I have challenges ahead and the reality is the next trip back will be for my funeral,” she said.
When you were saying good-bye, I heard you say, “I wish you enough.”  May I ask what that means?”
She began to smile.  “That’s a wish that has been handed down from other generations.  My parents used to say it to everyone.”  She paused a moment and looked up as if trying to remember it in detail and she smiled even more.
“When we said ‘I wish you enough’ we were wanting the other person to have a life filled with just enough good things to sustain them”.  Then turning toward me, she shared the following, reciting it from memory,
“I wish you enough sun to keep your attitude bright.
I wish you enough rain to appreciate the sun more.
I wish you enough happiness to keep your spirit alive.
I wish you enough pain so that the smallest joys in life appear much bigger.
I wish you enough gain to satisfy your wanting.
I wish you enough loss to appreciate all that you possess.
I wish you enough hellos to get you through the final good-bye.”
She then began to cry and walked away.
They say it takes a minute to find a special person.   An hour to appreciate them.  A day to love them.  And an entire life to forget them.
